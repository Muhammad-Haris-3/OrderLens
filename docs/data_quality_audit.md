# Blocking findings

## F-01 — `is_late` is measured in the wrong unit

**Grade: Blocking. This is the most consequential finding in the audit.**

### What was found

`order_estimated_delivery_date` is a **date**, stored at midnight. All 96,470
delivered orders have it at exactly `00:00:00` (A-10). `delivered_at` is a real
timestamp, and **not one delivery in the dataset** was recorded at exactly
midnight (A-10).

The Design Phase defines `delay_days = delivered_at − estimated_delivery_at` and
`is_late = delay_days > 0`. Applied to these two columns, that arithmetic
classifies an order as late if it arrives at *any* time on the promised day —
because every moment of the promised day is after the midnight the promise is
stored at.

Delivery hours are concentrated in the evening: 18:00 alone accounts for 9.99%
of deliveries, and 67.9% land between 15:00 and 23:00 (A-12). The affected group
is therefore not a rounding-error minority.

| Rule | Late orders | % late |
|---|---|---|
| `delivered_at > estimated_at` (timestamp) | 7,826 | 8.11% |
| `delivered_at::date > estimated_at::date` (calendar day) | 6,534 | 6.77% |

**1,292 orders were delivered on the day they were promised and counted as
late** (A-10).

### Why it matters — the cost, measured

The obvious objection is that 1,292 orders out of 96,470 is 1.3% and cannot
matter. It matters because those orders are not a random sample: they are the
best-performing members of the late group, and they land in the exact comparison
the project exists to make.

Mean review score, one review per order (A-11):

| Group | Orders | Mean review score |
|---|---|---|
| Late — timestamp rule | 7,661 | 2.565 |
| On time — timestamp rule | 88,163 | 4.294 |
| **Late — calendar-day rule** | **6,381** | **2.270** |
| **On time — calendar-day rule** | **89,443** | **4.290** |
| *Boundary group: delivered on the promised day* | *1,280* | *4.034* |

The boundary group scores **4.034** — it behaves like an on-time delivery, not
like a late one. Filing it under "late" pulls the late-group mean up from 2.270
to 2.565 and shrinks the measured gap:

| | Gap in mean review score |
|---|---|
| Timestamp rule | 1.729 |
| Calendar-day rule | **2.020** |

**The timestamp rule understates the effect of late delivery on satisfaction by
0.29 review points — 14.4% of the true gap.**

This is the number the entire project is built to estimate (BQ-2, BQ-3), and the
recommendation in M7 is costed from it. A 14% understatement propagates into
every downstream figure: the regression coefficient in M5, the target definition
in M6, and the projected return of the intervention in M7.

Nothing about this failure is visible. No test fails, no null appears, no row
count changes. The result is simply, quietly, 14% too small.

### Decision

> **`delay_days` and `is_late` are computed on calendar days, not timestamps.**
>
> ```sql
> delay_days = delivered_at::date - estimated_delivery_at::date   -- signed, whole days
> is_late    = delay_days > 0
> ```
>
> The promise made to the customer was a **day**. The comparison is made at the
> granularity the promise was made at. A parcel arriving at 18:00 on the day it
> was promised arrived on time, and the model must agree with the customer.

**Consequences for M3:** the data dictionary formulas for `delay_days` and
`is_late` are amended (both now read `::date`). `delivery_days`,
`seller_handover_days` and `carrier_transit_days` keep full timestamp precision
— they measure elapsed duration against another measurement, not against a
date-granular promise, so no unit mismatch exists there.

**Reported everywhere:** headline late rate is **6.77%**, not 8.11%.

## F-02 — "One review per order" is false

**Grade: Blocking. Resolves deferred decision D-1.**

### What was found

Both candidate keys for the reviews table fail (A-02, A-03):

| Key | Rows | Distinct | Excess |
|---|---|---|---|
| `review_id` | 99,224 | 98,410 | 814 |
| `order_id` | 99,224 | 98,673 | 551 |

The two failures have different causes, and the distinction decides the fix
(A-04):

- **789 `review_id` values appear against more than one `order_id`** — and in
  every single case the duplicated rows point at *different* orders and agree on
  the score. This is not a duplicated row. It is one satisfaction survey
  covering several orders by the same customer. Deleting these rows would
  discard real satisfaction data.
- **547 orders carry more than one review**, and **202 of them carry reviews
  that disagree on the score**. This is the genuine grain violation: the fact
  table joins on `order_id`, and without a rule, 551 orders would silently
  double-count.

No two rows in the table are identical across `(review_id, order_id,
review_score, review_creation_date)`, so nothing here is a straightforward
duplicate.

### Does the rule matter?

Measured before choosing, so the choice is evidence-led (A-05):

| Statistic | All rows | Keep latest | Keep earliest |
|---|---|---|---|
| Rows | 99,224 | 98,673 | 98,673 |
| Mean review score | 4.0864 | 4.0864 | 4.0872 |
| Low-score rate (≤2) | 14.689% | 14.689% | 14.671% |

The headline barely moves — 0.0008 of a review point between rules. **Reported
honestly: this decision does not change any published number.** It has to be
made anyway, because leaving it unmade means 551 orders are weighted twice in
every join, and an unstated weighting is worse than a stated one that happens
not to matter.

### Decision

> **`stg_order_reviews` has a grain of one row per `order_id`.**
>
> Where an order carries several reviews, keep the **latest by
> `review_answer_timestamp`**, tie-broken on `review_id` for determinism.
>
> ```sql
> select distinct on (order_id) ...
> order by order_id, review_answer_timestamp desc, review_id
> ```
>
> Latest, not earliest: where a customer reviewed the same order twice, the
> second response is the settled opinion. The tie-break exists so the model is
> reproducible — without it, two builds of the same data can disagree.
>
> `review_id` is **not** tested for uniqueness. It is genuinely not unique and
> never will be; a failing test that everyone learns to ignore is worse than no
> test. The uniqueness test goes on `order_id` **after** deduplication, where it
> asserts something true and load-bearing.

A `review_count` column is carried on `fct_orders` so the 547 multi-review
orders remain visible rather than silently collapsed.

## F-03 — Status and delivery timestamps contradict each other

**Grade: Blocking. Resolves deferred decision D-2.**

### What was found

Eight statuses exist (A-06). The contradictions run in both directions (A-07):

| Contradiction | Status | Orders |
|---|---|---|
| `status = 'delivered'` but no delivery timestamp | delivered | **8** |
| `status <> 'delivered'` but a delivery timestamp exists | canceled | **6** |

Both are traps for the obvious filter. Filtering on status alone admits 8 orders
with no delivery date, whose `delay_days` is null and whose presence makes the
on-time rate denominator wrong. Filtering on the timestamp alone admits 6 orders
that were delivered and then cancelled — a returned or refused parcel is a
delivery outcome, but it is not a satisfied sale.

Status also determines which timestamps exist at all: `shipped` orders (1,107)
have a carrier handover but never a delivery; `unavailable`, `invoiced`,
`processing`, `created` and `approved` orders never reach a carrier.

### Decision

> **Delivery analysis population:**
> ```sql
> order_status = 'delivered' AND delivered_at IS NOT NULL
> ```
> Both conditions, not either. This yields **96,470** orders.
>
> The 8 status-delivered-without-timestamp orders are excluded from delivery
> analysis and **retained in `fct_orders`** with null delivery measures. They are
> real orders with real revenue; deleting them would understate revenue to fix a
> delivery metric.
>
> The 6 delivered-then-cancelled orders are excluded from delivery *and*
> satisfaction analysis, and retained for cancellation analysis. A cancelled
> order is not a successful delivery regardless of what the timestamp says.
>
> `fct_orders` keeps **all 99,441 orders**. Delivery measures are null outside
> the eligible population. The filter lives in the analysis, not in the fact
> table — a fact table that has already dropped rows cannot answer "how many
> orders never arrived", which is BQ-1.

An `is_delivery_eligible` boolean is materialised on `fct_orders` so that every
consumer applies the same rule, rather than each re-deriving it and drifting.

## F-04 — 775 orders have no items

**Grade: Blocking. Resolves deferred decision D-4.**

### What was found

Referential integrity is clean in the child-to-parent direction. There are **no
orphans of any kind** (A-14): no items, payments or reviews without a parent
order; no orders without a customer; no items referencing an unknown product or
seller. Zero across the board.

The parent-to-child direction is where the interesting cases are:

| Gap | Orders |
|---|---|
| Orders with no items | 775 |
| Orders with no review | 768 |
| Orders with no payment | 1 |

These are not corruption. Broken down by status (A-15), 603 of the 775 are
`unavailable` and 164 are `canceled` — an order that could not be fulfilled has
nothing to itemise. Every one of them has a payment record, and 756 of them have
a customer review.

The single payment-less order is a delivered order with three items worth
R$143.46 — a genuinely missing payment record, one row in 99,441.

### Decision

> **`fct_orders` joins `stg_order_items` with a `LEFT JOIN`.** An inner join
> would delete 775 orders, and with them the entire population of "orders the
> platform failed to fulfil" — which is one of the operational failures this
> project exists to quantify (BQ-1).
>
> **`order_value` is NULL for these orders, not 0.** Zero is a claim that the
> order was worth nothing. Null is the truth: unknown, because it was never
> itemised. A zero would silently drag every average order value down.
>
> Relationship tests from `order_items`, `order_payments` and `order_reviews`
> **to** `fct_orders` are `error` severity — a child without a parent is
> corruption. There is no test in the reverse direction, because a childless
> parent is a business state, not a defect.

## F-05 — 623 products never reach an English category

**Grade: Blocking. Resolves deferred decision D-3.**

### What was found

First seen in M1 §5; quantified here. Of 32,951 products, 32,328 translate.
The 623-product gap has two distinct causes (A-16):

| Cause | Products | Order items | Orders | Item revenue |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| No category on the product at all | 610 | 1,603 | 1,451 | R$179,535.28 |
| Category exists, missing from the lookup | 13 | 24 | 22 | R$5,514.48 |

The lookup holds 71 categories; 73 distinct categories are actually in use. The
two missing ones are `portateis_cozinha_e_preparadores_de_alimentos` and
`pc_gamer` (A-17).

**An `INNER JOIN` on the category lookup would silently delete R$185,049 of item
revenue** across 1,473 orders — and the resulting category report would look
perfectly clean, because every row remaining in it would be correct.

### Decision

> **`dim_products` joins the category lookup with a `LEFT JOIN`, and degrades in
> three labelled steps:**
>
> ```sql
> coalesce(t.product_category_name_english,   -- 32,328 products
>          p.product_category_name,           -- 13 products, Portuguese retained
>          'uncategorised')                   -- 610 products
> ```
>
> A `category_source` column records which step produced the value:
> `translated` / `portuguese_fallback` / `missing`.
>
> The two causes are labelled differently on purpose. `pc_gamer` is a known
> category with a gap in the lookup table — the Portuguese name is accurate and
> usable, and blanking it would destroy information to tidy a join. A product
> with no category at all is missing data, and `'uncategorised'` says so out
> loud rather than hiding among real categories.
>
> `category_source` is not decoration: it lets M4 state what share of category
> revenue is attributable, instead of quietly reporting on 98% of it as though
> it were 100%.

## F-06 — The dataset does not cover the range it appears to

**Grade: Blocking. Resolves deferred decision D-5.**

### What was found

`min` and `max` of the purchase timestamp give 2016-09-04 to 2018-10-17 — a
25-month range. Monthly volume shows that range is not what it looks like
(A-18):

| Month | Orders | Delivered |
|---|---|---|
| 2016-09 | 4 | 1 |
| 2016-10 | 324 | 265 |
| **2016-11** | **absent entirely** | — |
| 2016-12 | 1 | 1 |
| 2017-01 … 2018-08 | 800 – 7,544 per month | 93.8% – 98.9% |
| **2018-09** | **16** | **0** |
| **2018-10** | **4** | **0** |

Three separate problems:

1. **A truncated tail.** The last 20 orders were placed but none was ever
   delivered — they were still in flight when the extract was taken. Their
   delivery outcome is *unknown*, not *bad*.
2. **A missing month.** November 2016 has no rows at all. A time series drawn
   from observed dates alone would join October 2016 straight to December 2016
   and draw a line through a month that never existed.
3. **A pilot period.** 2016 carries 329 orders in total against ~6,500/month
   through 2018. Including it in a trend chart shows explosive growth that is an
   artefact of the platform's own launch, not of anything operational.

### Anchor date

Recency for RFM must be measured from a fixed date, not `now()`. The candidates
(A-30):

| Candidate | Date | Orders in prior 30 days |
|---|---|---|
| max `order_purchase_timestamp` | 2018-10-17 | 8 |
| max `order_delivered_customer_date` | 2018-10-17 | 8 |
| **Last day of the last month with ≥1,000 orders** | **2018-08-31** | **6,201** |

Anchoring on the maximum purchase timestamp measures recency from a date on
which the platform was, as far as the data shows, barely trading. Every customer
gains 47 days of artificial staleness, and the most recent RFM segment is
populated by eight orders.

### Decisions

> **Analysis anchor date: 2018-08-31.** RFM recency is measured from the last
> day of the last month with full trading coverage, not from the maximum
> timestamp in a truncated tail. The 20 orders placed after this date are
> excluded from RFM and reported as excluded.
>
> **Trend and cohort window: 2017-01 through 2018-08.** Reported in every chart
> axis label, not buried in a footnote. 2016 is retained in the warehouse and
> excluded from trend analysis, with the exclusion stated.
>
> **`dim_dates` is generated as a gapless spine** across the full observed range
> — including November 2016. The gap must appear in a chart as a visible zero,
> because a date dimension built from observed order dates would silently omit
> it and no reader could tell.

## F-07 — Geolocation fans out 52.6×, and does not cover everyone

**Grade: Blocking. Confirms risk R-2 and adds a second problem.**

### What was found

R-2 restated as measurement (A-20): 1,000,163 rows across 19,015 ZIP prefixes —
a mean of **52.6 rows per prefix**, with one prefix carrying **1,146 rows**.
Joined un-aggregated to any fact table, this multiplies rows by roughly fifty
and inflates every revenue figure by the same factor.

The aggregation rule also needs defending, and the data defends it: **8,556 of
19,015 prefixes (45%) carry more than one spelling of their own city name**, and
8 prefixes span more than one state. A prefix does not agree with itself, so
`mode()` rather than `min()` or an arbitrary pick.

The coverage problem is new and was not anticipated (A-21):

| Side | Rows | Without coordinates | Distinct prefixes missing |
|---|---|---|---|
| Customers | 99,441 | 278 (0.28%) | 157 |
| Sellers | 3,095 | 7 (0.23%) | 7 |

### Decision

> **`stg_geolocation` aggregates to one row per ZIP prefix before anything joins
> to it** — mean latitude and longitude for the centroid, **modal** city and
> state, and `source_point_count` retained so a thinly-evidenced centroid can be
> spotted rather than trusted.
>
> `zip_prefix` carries a uniqueness test. That test is the assertion that R-2 is
> dead, and `fct_orders` carries a row-count equality test against `stg_orders`
> as the backstop.
>
> **Geography joins are `LEFT JOIN`.** 278 customers and 7 sellers have no
> coordinates; an inner join would remove them from the fact table entirely and
> every geographic total would under-report without any test noticing.
> Distance-based features in M6 are null for these rows and the model must
> handle null, not silently drop.

# Material findings

## F-08 — Lifecycle timestamps run backwards

**Grade: Material.** Among 96,478 delivered orders (A-09):

| Violation | Orders |
|---|---|
| Handed to carrier *before* the order was placed | 165 |
| Approved *after* being handed to carrier | 1,350 |
| Delivered *before* being handed to carrier | 23 |
| Approved before purchase / delivered before purchase | 0 |

The worst case hands a parcel to the carrier nearly six months before the
customer ordered it. These are data-entry errors, not physics.

**Decision:** `seller_handover_days` and `carrier_transit_days` are set to
**NULL** where the underlying interval is negative, not clamped to zero. A
clamped zero asserts an instantaneous handover; null asserts that the recorded
timestamps cannot support the measure — which is the truth. A dbt test asserts
both measures are null-or-non-negative, so a future change cannot reintroduce a
negative duration into a segment mean. `delivery_days` and `delay_days` are
unaffected: no order is delivered before it was purchased.

The 1,350 approved-after-carrier rows are left alone — payment approval and
physical dispatch are recorded by different systems and a small overlap is
expected, not corrupt.

## F-09 — 4,795 reviews were written before the parcel arrived

**Grade: Material. This is a validity finding, not a cleanliness one.**

Of 99,224 reviews joined to an order (A-27): 2,865 sit on orders that were never
delivered, 8,320 surveys were *created* before delivery, and **4,795 (4.98% of
reviews on delivered orders) were answered before the parcel arrived.**

The satisfaction survey is evidently triggered at dispatch, not at delivery. A
review answered before delivery **cannot be a response to the delivery
experience** — the customer had not yet had it.

**Decision:** these reviews are retained (removing them would bias the sample
toward customers who waited). A `reviewed_before_delivery` flag is carried on
`fct_orders`, and:

- **M5** runs the headline test both with and without them. If the estimate
  moves materially, both numbers are published — a sensitivity analysis, not a
  choice between them.
- **M6** excludes them from training. Their outcome is not caused by the
  features being used to predict it.
- **FR-12's limitations statement** records this explicitly. It is a structural
  limitation of the measurement instrument, not something cleaning can fix.

## F-10 — Payments exceed item totals on 264 orders

**Grade: Material.** Across 98,665 orders with both (A-24): 98,362 reconcile to
the cent, 264 have payments exceeding item value (max +R$182.81), and 39 fall
short (max −R$51.62). Net difference R$2,870.39 on R$15.8M — 0.018%.

The asymmetry is the point: overpayments outnumber underpayments 6.8 to 1,
exactly the signature of installment interest. Credit cards run to 24
installments (A-25).

**Decision:** the data dictionary's rule stands and is now evidenced rather than
asserted — **`order_value` comes from `order_items`, never from
`order_payments`.** `fct_payments` stays a separate model at payment grain;
payment-method analysis reads it, revenue analysis does not.

Degenerate payment rows are recorded rather than removed: 3 rows of type
`not_defined` with zero value, 6 zero-value vouchers, and 2 credit-card rows
with zero installments. All 11 are kept, and the `payment_type`
`accepted_values` test **includes `not_defined`** — the test documents what the
source contains, and a test that fails on a known, harmless value trains people
to ignore failures.

## F-11 — Review scores are severely skewed

**Grade: Material.** 57.78% of reviews are 5-star; 11.51% are 1-star; the middle
is thin (A-28). The distribution is bimodal, not central.

**Decision:** this confirms SRS §6.3 with numbers and settles the M5 method
choice. Mean-based summaries are reported *alongside* the distribution, never
alone, and hypothesis tests are **rank-based (Mann-Whitney U with rank-biserial
effect size)** rather than t-tests. A t-test on a bimodal 1–5 ordinal scale
assumes a normality that A-28 rules out.

Comment coverage is inversely related to score: 76.5% of 1-star reviews carry a
message against 35.9% of 5-star ones. Any future text analysis is therefore
sampling complaints, not opinions — recorded here so that is not discovered
later and mistaken for a finding.

## F-12 — 1,278 orders span several sellers

**Grade: Material.** 98.7% of orders involve one seller; 1,219 involve two, and
59 involve three or more (A-29).

`fct_orders` holds one delivery outcome per order, but for these 1,278 orders
"which seller caused the delay" has no single answer.

**Decision:** seller-level ranking in BQ-4 is built from `fct_order_items`, at
item grain, and single-seller orders only. The multi-seller orders are reported
as an explicit exclusion with their count and revenue share, rather than
attributed to every seller involved — which would double-count the damage and
inflate exactly the ranking the recommendation depends on.

## F-13 — 42 geolocation points are not in Brazil

**Grade: Material.** 42 rows fall outside Brazil's bounding box (lat −34 to +6,
lng −74 to −33), affecting 21 ZIP prefixes (A-22). State codes themselves are
clean: 27 distinct values, all two characters, matching Brazil's 26 states plus
the Federal District.

These 42 rows are averaged into prefix centroids, so one point in the wrong
hemisphere drags an entire ZIP prefix off the map along with any distance
derived from it.

**Decision:** `stg_geolocation` filters out-of-bounds points **before**
averaging. This is the one place in the pipeline where a row is discarded rather
than flagged, and the justification is specific: an impossible coordinate
contributes nothing recoverable to a centroid, and 42 rows out of 1,000,163
(0.004%) cannot meaningfully thin any prefix. The count of discarded points is
logged as a model-level test so the number cannot grow unnoticed.

# Noted findings

## F-14 — Repeat customers exist (risk R-1, confirmed again)

99,441 `customer_id` values collapse to 96,096 people. **2,997 people placed
more than one order**, one of them 17 (A-19). Keying retention on `customer_id`
would report a 100% first-time customer base.

**Decision:** unchanged from the Design Phase — `dim_customers` keys on
`customer_unique_id`, and carries the bespoke test asserting repeat customers
*exist*. A schema test cannot catch this: the wrong key is still perfectly
unique. Only an assertion about meaning catches it.

## F-15 — Product attributes are almost complete

32,951 products: 610 missing category, 610 missing photo count, 2 missing weight
and dimensions, 4 with a recorded weight of zero, none with a zero dimension
(A-26).

**Decision:** the 4 zero weights are set to null in `stg_products` — a product
does not weigh nothing, so zero is an unrecorded value wearing a number's
clothing, and it would corrupt any density feature derived from it. The 6
missing values are left null. M6 handles them as missing rather than imputed;
imputing across 6 rows would invent data to no benefit.

## F-16 — Prices and freight are clean

112,650 items: **no zero or negative prices, no negative freight** (A-23). Prices
run R$0.85 to R$6,735.00 (median R$74.99). 383 items ship free across 339 orders.

**Decision:** no action. Free freight is a promotion, not an error, and
`freight_ratio` is guarded by `nullif(order_item_total, 0)` against a division
that would otherwise abort the model build.

## F-17 — Missing timestamps are all explained

Delivery timestamps are missing on 2.982% of orders, carrier timestamps on
1.793%, approval on 0.161%. No missing `order_id`, `customer_id`,
`order_status`, `order_purchase_timestamp` or `order_estimated_delivery_date`
(A-08). Every missing value is SQL NULL; there are no empty strings masquerading
as data.

**Decision:** no action. Cross-referenced against A-06, the missing values are
fully accounted for by order status — an undelivered order has no delivery
timestamp because it has not been delivered. This is the dataset behaving
correctly, and it is recorded so that "2.98% missing" is never mistaken for a
data-quality problem later.

## Deferred decisions — all resolved

The Design Phase §10 deferred five decisions to this audit. None is left open.

| # | Question | Resolution | Finding |
|---|---|---|---|
| **D-1** | How duplicated is `review_id`, and what is the dedup rule? | Grain is `order_id`, not `review_id`. Keep latest by `review_answer_timestamp`, tie-break `review_id`. `review_id` never tested for uniqueness. | F-02 |
| **D-2** | Which statuses are eligible for delivery analysis? | `order_status = 'delivered' AND delivered_at IS NOT NULL` — 96,470 orders. All 99,441 retained in `fct_orders` with null delivery measures outside that set. | F-03 |
| **D-3** | How are unmapped-category products labelled? | Three-step coalesce: English → Portuguese → `'uncategorised'`, with a `category_source` column recording which step fired. | F-05 |
| **D-4** | Are there orders with no items, or reviews with no order? | 775 orders with no items (a real business state — `LEFT JOIN`, null order value). Zero orphan reviews, items or payments — reverse-direction relationship tests are `error` severity. | F-04 |
| **D-5** | What is the analysis anchor date? | **2018-08-31**. Trend and cohort window 2017-01 to 2018-08. | F-06 |

## What the audit did not find

Stated because absence of evidence is a result, and because a report that only
lists problems invites the question of what was skipped:

- **No corruption.** Zero orphan rows in any child-to-parent direction (A-14).
- **No row loss.** All nine tables still reconcile to their source files (A-01).
- **No duplicate rows.** Every table's declared grain holds except reviews (A-02).
- **No impossible values.** No negative prices, freight or payments; no
  out-of-range review scores; no malformed state codes (A-23, A-25, A-28).
- **No hidden encoding damage.** The UTF-8 BOM found in M1 was the only one;
  city names are consistently cased and no field carries empty strings in place
  of nulls (A-08).

The dataset is clean. What it is not is **self-explanatory** — and every
blocking finding above is a place where a correct-looking rule produces a wrong
answer with nothing failing to warn you.
